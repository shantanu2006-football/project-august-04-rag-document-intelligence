Distributed Systems: Consistency and the CAP Theorem

A distributed system is a collection of independent computers that appears to its
users as a single coherent system. Because the machines communicate over an
unreliable network, distributed systems must make explicit trade-offs that a
single-machine program never has to consider.

The CAP theorem, formalized by Eric Brewer and later proven by Gilbert and Lynch,
states that a distributed data store can provide at most two of the following
three guarantees simultaneously: Consistency, Availability, and Partition
tolerance. Consistency means every read receives the most recent write or an
error. Availability means every request receives a non-error response, without
the guarantee that it contains the most recent write. Partition tolerance means
the system continues to operate despite an arbitrary number of messages being
dropped or delayed by the network between nodes.

Because network partitions are a fact of life in any system that spans more than
one machine, real systems must choose between consistency and availability when
a partition occurs. Systems like ZooKeeper and etcd favor consistency (CP): they
will refuse to serve a request rather than risk returning stale data. Systems
like Cassandra and DynamoDB favor availability (AP) by default: they will serve
a response from whatever replica is reachable, and reconcile conflicting writes
later using techniques such as vector clocks or last-write-wins.

Consensus algorithms are the mechanism most CP systems use to agree on a single
value despite failures. Paxos, published by Leslie Lamport, was the first
widely analyzed consensus protocol, but its correctness proofs are notoriously
difficult to follow, which motivated the design of Raft. Raft achieves the same
guarantees as Paxos but decomposes the problem into leader election, log
replication, and safety, making it far easier to implement correctly. Both
protocols require a majority (quorum) of nodes to agree before a value is
considered committed, which is why clusters are typically sized with an odd
number of nodes such as three or five.

Beyond consensus, distributed systems rely on idempotent operations, retries
with exponential backoff, and timeouts to remain correct in the presence of
duplicate messages and slow nodes. Idempotency in particular is essential: if a
client cannot tell whether a request succeeded before a timeout, it must be safe
to retry that request without causing a double effect, such as charging a
payment twice.
